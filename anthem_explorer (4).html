A Digital Musicology Project

Introduction

National anthems are one of the most universal musical forms in the world — nearly every country has one, and yet each carries a distinct story about how a nation sees itself. This project maps and explores 197 national anthems across time, asking: when were they adopted, what musical form do they take, and what political moment gave birth to them?

By visualizing anthems geographically and chronologically, patterns emerge that no single country's history reveals on its own — the wave of post-colonial independence anthems across Africa and Asia in the 1960s, the cluster of post-communist transitions after 1991, the enduring presence of royal hymns across monarchies old and new.

How to Use This Map

  • Click any dot on the map to see that country's current anthem, composer, adoption date, and a link to listen on YouTube.
  • Drag the timeline slider to travel through history — watch anthems appear as countries adopt them.
  • Filter by Musical Style or Political Context using the buttons at the top to isolate patterns across the map.
  • Toggle "anthem changes" to highlight only the 75 countries that have changed their anthem at least once — click those dots to see the full history.

Data Sources

The core dataset was compiled from two Wikipedia sources: the List of National Anthems and the List of Former National Anthems, which together provided anthem titles, adoption dates, composers, lyricists, and audio lengths for 197 current and 137 historical anthems. Geographic coordinates were added manually for map placement.

The Wikipedia former anthems table includes de facto, unofficial, and occupation-era anthems alongside official ones. These were filtered out manually, keeping only anthems with clear official adoption dates and government recognition. Disputed cases (e.g. anthems used during civil conflicts, short-lived transitional anthems) were reviewed individually.

Classification Methodology

Two analytical categories were added to the dataset that do not appear in the original sources:

Musical Style classifies each anthem into one of five types: Hymn, March, Folk Song, Royal Anthem, or Revolutionary Song. These classifications draw on musicological conventions and were assigned based on compositional form, tempo character, and historical context of each anthem.

Political Context classifies the political moment of each anthem's adoption into one of six categories: Independence, Monarchy, Revolution, Post-Communist Transition, Republic/Federation, or Resistance/Liberation. These were assigned based on the historical circumstances of each country at the time of adoption.

Both classification layers were developed using Claude (Anthropic), an AI assistant, as a research and categorization tool. All AI-generated classifications were reviewed and corrected against historical sources. The use of AI as a DH research tool is documented here as part of the project's methodology.

How This Was Built

Step 1 — Data collection. Two datasets were sourced from Wikipedia: the List of National Anthems (current anthems) and the List of Former National Anthems (historical ones). Both were downloaded as CSV files — structured tables with columns for country, anthem name, adoption date, composer, and lyricist.

Step 2 — Data cleaning. The raw CSVs were messy: split rows, duplicate entries, inconsistent formatting, and mixed de facto and unofficial anthems alongside official ones. Each file was cleaned manually and with scripting — standardizing country names, extracting clean year ranges from ambiguous date strings, and filtering out non-official entries (occupation anthems, exile governments, de facto-only anthems).

Step 3 — Merging and enriching. The two cleaned datasets were merged into a single JSON file, linking each country's current anthem with its full historical record. Geographic coordinates were added for every country to enable map placement. Two new analytical columns — Musical Style and Political Context — were classified using Claude (Anthropic) as a research tool and reviewed manually.

Step 4 — Visualization. The website uses three open-source tools to display the data. Leaflet.js is a JavaScript library that places interactive dots on a map, handles zoom and click behavior, and shows popups — it's the engine behind the map interactivity. OpenStreetMap provides the underlying geographic data (coastlines, borders, country names), maintained freely by a global community. Carto renders that data into the styled map tiles you see as the background. Together they form the map layer; the data, filters, timeline, and detail panel were built on top.

Step 5 — Assembly and iteration. Claude was used to assemble the HTML, CSS, and JavaScript into a working website based on the data and design decisions made throughout the project. Edits to layout, color, filtering logic, zoom behavior, and the methods panel were made iteratively through the same process.

Built as a final project for a Digital Humanities course in Digital Musicology. Data compiled and cleaned from Wikipedia. Classifications developed with AI assistance and manual review. Visualization built in HTML, CSS, and JavaScript using Leaflet.js.
